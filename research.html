Research

Chromatin architecture

Software for the analysis, visualization, and prediction of 3D genome structure. Much of the analysis work is described in the doctoral thesis Approaches to analysis of chromosome conformation capture data (University of Freiburg, 2022).

Why chromatin architecture matters

A human cell packs about two meters of DNA into a nucleus a few micrometers across. The folding is not random. Chromosomes separate into active and inactive compartments, these are subdivided into topologically associating domains (TADs) of some hundred kilobases, and within the domains loops bring distant elements into contact, typically anchored by CTCF and cohesin.

This structure is part of gene regulation. An enhancer can only act on a promoter it physically meets, and domain boundaries limit which promoters that can be. When a boundary is lost or a rearrangement moves an enhancer into another domain, genes can be switched on in the wrong context. A known example from hematology is acute myeloid leukemia with inv(3) or t(3;3), where a GATA2 enhancer is relocated and activates the oncogene MECOM (EVI1). The linear sequence alone does not show such effects, the 3D structure does.

Hi-C measures this structure. Pairs of DNA fragments that are close in the nucleus are ligated and sequenced, and the counts are collected in an interaction matrix: the genome is cut into bins of, for example, 10 kb, and entry (i, j) is the number of contacts observed between bin i and bin j.

HiCExplorer

HiCExplorer covers the path from mapped reads to interpretable results: building and correcting the interaction matrix, quality control, detection of compartments, TADs, and loops, comparison of samples, capture Hi-C analysis, and plotting. It runs on the command line and in Galaxy.

pyGenomeTracks

pyGenomeTracks draws genome browser style figures from a configuration file: Hi-C matrices, TADs, loops, bigwig signals, and gene annotations as stacked tracks over the same region. scHiCExplorer: single-cell Hi-C and MinHash clustering

Bulk Hi-C averages over millions of cells. Single-cell Hi-C gives one matrix per cell, which makes it possible to group cells by how their chromatin is folded, for example by cell cycle stage or cell type. The difficulty is the data: a single cell yields few contacts, so each matrix is almost empty, and to cluster the cells every matrix is flattened into one row of a cells-by-bin-pairs matrix. At 10 kb resolution this matrix has up to billions of columns, and computing all pairwise distances between cells is not feasible in memory.

The approach in scHiCExplorer (Wolff et al., Bioinformatics 2021) avoids the full distance computation with MinHash, a locality-sensitive hashing technique. Each cell is treated as the set of its non-empty bin pairs. A hash function assigns every bin pair a number, and only the smallest number per cell is kept. Repeating this with many different hash functions gives each cell a short signature. The probability that two cells agree in one signature position equals the Jaccard similarity of their contact sets, so cells with similar folding collide often and unrelated cells rarely. Collisions are counted to find the likely nearest neighbors of each cell, only these candidates are compared exactly, and the resulting approximate k-nearest-neighbor graph is clustered with spectral clustering.

On a dataset of 2600 cells at 10 kb resolution the method needed 40 GB of memory, while the approaches it was compared with could not be computed with 1 TB, and the clusters compared favorably in how well they separated the cells by their folding. The nearest neighbor search goes back to the master thesis Approximate nearest neighbor query methods for large-scale structured datasets (University of Freiburg, 2016) and is available on its own as sparse-neighbors-search, and the companion scool format stores thousands of single-cell matrices in one file.

Hi-cGAN: predicting Hi-C matrices

Hi-C is expensive and needs many cells, so for most cell types and for patient samples no matrix exists, while ChIP-seq tracks of histone marks and architectural proteins often do. Since domains and loops are tied to these factors, a part of the matrix should be predictable from them.

Hi-cGAN (Krauth et al., preprint 2026) treats this as an image-to-image translation problem and uses a conditional generative adversarial network in the style of pix2pix. A window slides along the chromosome. For each window the binned ChIP-seq tracks are embedded into a two-dimensional image, and a convolutional generator translates it into the contact submatrix of that window. During training a second network, the discriminator, receives the tracks together with either the measured or the generated submatrix and learns to tell them apart. The generator is trained to fool the discriminator and at the same time to stay close to the measured values, which pushes it towards matrices that are both accurate and look like real Hi-C data instead of a blurred average. For prediction only the generator is needed: the windows are predicted along each chromosome and combined into a whole-genome matrix in cool format, at bin sizes between 2 and 25 kb. DNA sequence is not used.

Two input tracks capture most of what the factors can explain, and which ones are most informative depends on the resolution: CTCF and the cohesin subunits at 5 to 10 kb, active histone marks at 25 kb. The predicted maps describe structure at the scale of domains. Boundaries and loops called from them agree less well with the measurement, and methods that read DNA sequence reach higher agreement. Applying a model to a cell type it has not seen lowers the agreement moderately.

Clinical workflows

Software for sequencing-based hematology diagnostics and the lab work around it, with the aim of replacing manual steps by reproducible and traceable ones.

SampleSync

Before a sample is sequenced, its DNA concentration has to be measured, because library preparation needs a defined amount of input DNA. The standard instrument for this is the Qubit, a benchtop fluorometer: a dye that only fluoresces when bound to DNA is added to a small aliquot of the sample, and the measured fluorescence is converted into a concentration using two or three standards. The instrument is accurate, but it knows nothing about the sample. It shows a number per tube and exports a list of readings with timestamps, and which reading belongs to which tube is usually written down by hand. With dozens of tubes per run this is slow and a source of sample mix-ups.

SampleSync links the readings to tube barcodes without any connection to the instrument. The operator scans a tube's barcode, measures it, scans the next one, and so on. Afterwards the CSV export of the instrument is loaded and the two timelines, scans and readings, are aligned by their relative timing. The clocks of computer and instrument do not need to agree, the offset is estimated. If the alternation of scan and measurement is broken, for example a tube was scanned but never measured, the affected rows are flagged instead of being silently shifted. The result is a table of barcode, concentration, operator, and status. SampleSync is a small desktop application for Windows, Linux, and macOS that needs only Python.

Agentic AI translations to bare-metal C++

Much widely used genomics software is written in Java or Python and was never optimized for today's data volumes. These projects use agentic AI to translate such tools to bare-metal C++ for performance: lower memory use and shorter run times, with results validated against the original implementation.

falcoAmadeus

Quality control is the first step of every sequencing analysis, and FastQC is the tool almost everyone uses for it. It is written in Java and processes a file with a single thread. falco is a faster C++ reimplementation with the same output. falcoAmadeus is a fork of falco that reads and analyses FASTQ, gzipped FASTQ, SAM, and BAM files in parallel, and produces the same result with any number of threads. It writes the FastQC text format that downstream tools such as MultiQC expect, plus an interactive HTML report. It is available from Bioconda.

vardictcpp

VarDict is a variant caller that is widely used for targeted and deep sequencing, as in leukemia diagnostics. The Java implementation needs a lot of memory, which limits how many samples can run side by side. vardictcpp is a C++ port that needs a fraction of the memory and runs several times faster. On five public whole-exome datasets the output is identical to VarDictJava 1.8.3, line by line.
